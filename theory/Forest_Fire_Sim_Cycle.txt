Yep — makes total sense. You’re basically asking for a coupled system:

Tree spacing / stand density affects disease transmission (via contact + humidity/microclimate + dispersal pathways),

and it also affects fire behavior (ladder fuels, canopy bulk density, surface fuels),

while thinning / trimming can push risk down… but in some regimes it can also push parts of risk up if it increases dryness/wind or leaves surface fuels.

Below is a clean way to “spice it up” mathematically, plus some real-world spacing anchors.

1) Real-world spacing anchors (order-of-magnitude)

Plantations commonly start around ~3 m × 3 m (≈ 1,111 stems/ha) as a widely cited baseline; tighter spacings exist for certain objectives, but <3×3 is often treated as “dense.”

Thinning targets (example guidance) often translate to leaving trees ~13–15 ft apart (≈ 4–4.6 m) depending on site quality, which is basically “reduce density / increase spacing as stands mature.”

Disease + spacing: there’s empirical work showing wider spacing can materially reduce disease incidence/severity (example: wattle rust case showed substantially lower incidence/severity at wider spacing vs narrow).
More broadly, plantation/agroforest literature supports that spacing alters disease dynamics by changing contact rates and microclimate.

(We should ignore the Facebook “rules of thumb” style sources here — the above are the sturdier anchors.)
